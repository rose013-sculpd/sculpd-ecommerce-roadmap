01 · Executive summary

One person, five storefronts, a department's worth of remit

The problem is not that you are disorganised. The board in front of me is neat. The problem is that a list of tasks is not an operating system — it has no intake, no capacity model and no priority signal, so it can only ever be as reliable as the amount you can hold in your head. You have now passed that point. Everything below follows from that one diagnosis.

Where you actually are

You are the entire ecommerce function for a five-storefront Shopify estate (UK, US, EU, CA, AU). In most businesses at Sculpd's stage this job spec is filled by three people: a commercial ecommerce manager who owns roadmap, merchandising and reporting; a Shopify developer who owns theme, features and bugs; and an optimisation specialist who owns SEO, CRO, analytics and speed. You are all three, plus the QA function, plus the release manager.

The evidence that this is now costing you: six items in progress against a capacity of one; a card 25 days overdue and another 7 days overdue; two container cards carrying 11 and 14 comments of buried decisions; and eleven job-spec responsibilities that have never been ticketed at all.

The five-times multiplier

Every structural problem here is multiplied by five, because every change is currently made five times. Your release log shows it plainly: the same GeoRedirect v2 fix, the same DOM bloat fix, the same HP collection price fix, deployed store by store over days.

This is why the shared master theme is not one initiative among ten. It is the only initiative that changes the denominator. Until it exists, your effective capacity is one fifth of what the org thinks it is buying, and every process improvement in this document is worth five times less than it should be.

There is no front door

Requests arrive in Slack from Guy, Giles, Matt and Alix, across two timezones, and become work without ever becoming a ticket. Work that enters through a side door cannot be prioritised, estimated or declined — it can only be absorbed.

BOTTLENECK 02

Everything is Medium

Nine of the visible cards are Medium. "Medium" is not a priority, it is the absence of one. When every card is equally important, sequencing collapses back into whoever asked most recently — which is exactly the failure mode the board was meant to prevent.

BOTTLENECK 03

Six things in progress

Six parallel items for one person means six context loads, six half-finished branches and nothing crossing the line. Throughput for a solo operator is governed almost entirely by work-in-progress limits, and yours is currently three times too high.

BOTTLENECK 04

Why the existing process will not scale. A to-do list scales linearly with attention: it works right up until the list is longer than what you can hold in your head, and then it fails silently. The failure mode is not that work gets slow — it is that important work becomes invisible, which is precisely what has happened to peak readiness, the analytics baseline, gifting and localisation. Adding more categories to the same board makes this worse, not better, because classification was never the problem. The board is missing an intake, a capacity model and a priority signal, and no amount of tidying will supply those.

02 · Responsibility analysis

Nine pillars, four modes of work

The job spec lists nine areas. Grouping by subject matter — SEO here, CRO there — is how you end up with fourteen categories and a filter you stop maintaining. The more useful grouping for one person is by how the work behaves: what it needs from your attention, how it can be scheduled, and what it costs to interrupt. Four modes cover everything in the spec.

Build

Theme development, feature work, bundles, multibuy, gift builder, checkout and PDP enhancements, integrations.

Behaves like: long, blockable, expensive to interrupt. Needs 3–4 hour uninterrupted blocks or it costs double. Should never be scheduled in the same block as anything reactive.

Optimise

CRO and A/B testing, technical SEO, AI search, structured data, site speed, accessibility, analytics and tracking.

Behaves like: hypothesis-driven and measurable, with long feedback loops. Highly batchable — these all use the same tools and the same headspace, so they belong on one day, not four.

Operate

Bug fixing, QA, releases across five stores, app stack, merchandising, campaign builds, launch support, department requests.

Behaves like: rhythmic and partly unpredictable. Must be time-boxed into a lane, because it will otherwise expand to fill whatever space it finds. This is the mode that eats your build time.

Steer

Roadmap ownership, prioritisation, reporting, documentation, research, trend monitoring, stakeholder communication.

Behaves like: cheap in hours, catastrophic when skipped. Four hours a week. The first thing sacrificed under pressure and the reason the board drifted from reality in the first place.

Site speed lives in three pillars at once

It appears under Website Optimisation, SEO, and implicitly under Website Operations. Run it as one programme with one measurement (mobile LCP and INP per store, from your release log) and let it report into all three. Three separate speed workstreams is how you get three separate backlogs of the same fixes.

QA is named three times, is one function

"Manage implementation, QA and launch", "theme maintenance, QA, bug tracking", and campaign go-live checks. That is one QA checklist with three triggers: feature release, campaign release, peak pass. Write it once, automate it, run it three ways.

Merchandising and Campaign Support are one muscle

Homepage merchandising, featured collections, landing pages, seasonal merchandising, promotions, launches — same skills, same assets, same design dependency, different tempo. Merge into a single Trading workstream with a calendar, not two categories with a queue.

Structured data spans SEO and AI Search

Your AU pilot already proved this: Organization, Product, HowTo, llms.txt serve both classic rich results and AI surfaces. One schema workstream in the shared theme, rolled out per store as a property — not an SEO project and a separate AI project.

Analytics is both an input and an output

It feeds CRO hypotheses and roadmap prioritisation, and it is also the reporting deliverable. Build it once as a pipeline — weekly metric snapshot per store into a Notion database — and both uses are served from the same 20 minutes.

International is not a category — it is a dimension

This is the single most important reframe in this section. "International Sites" as a workstream implies a separate backlog. In reality store scope multiplies every other category. Make it a multi-select property on every ticket (UK · US · EU · CA · AU · Shared) and your board can finally answer the question that currently costs you hours: did this actually ship to AU?

What naturally belongs together. SEO + AI Search (same crawl, same schema, same templates). CRO + Analytics (one cannot function without the other). Bugs + QA + Releases (the same five-store deploy motion). Merchandising + Campaigns (Trading). Roadmap + Reporting (the same monthly artefact, pointed in two directions). That collapses nine pillars into five working surfaces, which is a number one person can actually hold.

Four things I would tell the client outright

International localisation cannot happen in H2

It is a named pillar of the job spec — translation workflows, localisation, regional merchandising — and it is the one pillar with no H2 coverage at all. Doing it properly means a translation tool, a content workflow and per-region merchandising rules: 16h+ before it produces anything. Say this out loud in August rather than letting it look covered. A gap you have named is a resourcing conversation. A gap you have hidden is a performance conversation.

Accessibility is a compliance question, not a backlog item

The European Accessibility Act applies to ecommerce services in the EU, and the UK has its own equalities obligations. That makes accessibility on the EU and UK storefronts a question for whoever owns legal risk at Sculpd, not something that waits its turn behind a multibuy block. Recommended action: raise it as a decision at the next fortnightly, scope an audit, and either get it resourced or get a written acceptance of the risk.

Subscriptions and personalisation are new business models

They sit in the spec next to "product page enhancements" as though they were the same size of thing. They are not. Each needs a commercial case, an app or platform decision, an ops process and probably a contractor. Treat them as business cases you write, not builds you deliver — that is genuinely the Ecommerce Manager half of this role, and it is 4h each rather than 20h each.

"Monitor emerging search trends" needs a box or it becomes nothing

Open-ended research responsibilities have two stable states for a busy person: zero hours or five. Give it one hour, Friday afternoon, timeboxed, with the output being a single line in the learnings log. That is enough to stay current and small enough to survive a bad week.

The honest bottom line. With the shared theme, the four priority automations and a working priority ladder, one person can credibly deliver roughly 70% of this job specification to a good standard. Trying to deliver 100% produces five workstreams at 60% quality and a person who leaves in February. The 30% that does not fit should be named, agreed and either deferred to 2027 or resourced — and the utilisation data from this system is what turns that from an opinion into an argument.
04 · Priority framework

Six levels, published SLAs, and one rule that makes it work

A priority scale only works if it is scarce at the top. The reason your current board has nine Mediums is that there was no cost to choosing Medium. This ladder attaches a promise to every level — and because the promise is public, over-using P0 costs something.

The one rule everything else depends on: requesters supply impact and deadline. You assign priority. Not because you know better than Giles what matters commercially, but because priority is a statement about sequence across all work, and you are the only person who can see all the work. Without this rule, P0 inflates within a month and the ladder is decoration. Put this sentence in the Notion header and in your Slack channel topic.
P0Ack: 1h in overlap
Fix: same day

05 · Work categorisation

Ten categories, and everything else becomes a property

You proposed fourteen categories. I would cut to ten — and the cut matters, because every category is a filter you have to maintain and a decision you have to make at 4pm on a Friday. The things I have removed are not lost; they become properties, which is where they were always more useful.

SEO & AI Search Technical SEO, on-page, metadata, schema, internal linking, llms.txt, AI surfaces. One category, not two.

What I removed, and where it went

Accessibility→ a tag. It applies across Build, Trading and Performance. As a category it fragments; as a tag it is reportable.
Client Requests→ a Source property. Who asked is not what kind of work it is. Keeping it as a category means an identical bug is filed in two places depending on who noticed it.
Experiments→ folded into CRO, which is what an experiment is here.
Technical Debt→ P5 priority inside Ops & Maintenance. Debt is a priority statement, not a subject.
Administration→ recurring calendar blocks. If it is not worth a ticket, it should not have one.
International→ Store Scope, the most valuable property on the board.
The test for a category: would you ever want to see only this, and would two different people file the same ticket into the same one? Accessibility fails the second test. Store scope fails the first — you always want it alongside something else. That is how you tell a category from a property.

06 · Notion operating system

Six databases, one teamspace, three permissions to ask for

You said you have limited control of a shared workspace but could ask for more. Do not ask for the workspace — ask for one teamspace you own. That is a small, specific, easy-to-grant request, and it is all this design needs. Everything below lives inside it, and the rest of the company sees only three read-only views.

And stay on Notion. You had the option to move to Linear or similar. Linear is a better pure engineering tracker, but two thirds of this role is not engineering — it is roadmap, reporting, documentation, learnings and stakeholder-facing views, which is exactly where Notion is strong and Linear is not. More importantly: a tool migration in August, before the biggest quarter of the year, is a risk with no revenue attached. Revisit in January if the sprint mechanics genuinely chafe.

The six databases

Each exists because the things inside it share a half-life. That is the only good reason to separate databases, and it is why one board was failing.

01 · Inbox half-life: hours

Four properties and nothing else: Title · Source · Requester · Raw note. No category, no priority, no estimate. The entire design goal is that capturing something costs under ten seconds, because anything slower and you will not do it when you are mid-build.

Emptied: once a day at the 15:30 triage block. Empty by end of day, every day. This is the only queue in the system with a zero target.

02 · Delivery half-life: days–weeks

The working database. Every property from §05, every status from §07. One row = one shippable thing with a definition of done. If it cannot be finished inside one sprint, it is not a Delivery item — it is a Programme with children.

Expected size: 40–70 live rows. If it passes 100, the aging rule is not being applied.

03 · Programmes half-life: quarters

8–12 rows, ever. Shared Theme Architecture · CRO Programme · Site Speed · AI Search · Peak Readiness · Bundles & AOV Mechanics · Analytics & Reporting · AI Automation. Each has an owner, a quarterly goal, a success measure and a relation to its Delivery children with a % complete rollup.

Fixes: the "container card that never closes" problem. A programme is allowed to stay open. A ticket is not.

04 · Sprints half-life: two weeks

One row per sprint. Dates, committed hours, completed hours, carry-over, reactive hours actually spent, and one field for the retro's single process change. Rollups from Delivery do the arithmetic.

Why it matters: after six sprints this database is a dataset. It tells you your true velocity and your true reactive rate — the two numbers that turn "I am overloaded" into a chart.

05 · Releases replaces the Google Sheet

Theme name, date, stores deployed, tickets included (relation), LCP/INP after, rollback note, and a "deployed to" multi-select that shows at a glance which of the five stores are behind. Your current sheet already holds all of this — it is just in a different tool from the tickets it references, which is why the Notion links have to be pasted by hand.

Immediate win: one view answers "which stores have not had the AI Search deploy yet" — currently a manual read across five sheet tabs.

06 · Learnings & Decisions half-life: years

CRO test results, the bundles findings, architecture decisions, "why we removed PageFly", "why Buy X Get Y needs manual add". One row, one decision, one paragraph, tagged and searchable.

Doubles as: the Documentation deliverable in the job spec, the A/B learnings log the spec explicitly asks for, and your bus-factor insurance. Three obligations, one database.

Optional seventh — Metrics. Five rows a week (one per store): sessions, CVR, AOV, RPV, mobile LCP. Ten minutes on a Friday, or automated. It powers every chart in your reporting pack and gives you the baselines the job spec is written against. If you only add one thing beyond the six, add this.

Quick capture — three routes, all under ten seconds

1. Slack → the Notion Slack integration, "Add to Inbox" on any message. Captures the thread link and the requester automatically. This is the one that matters most, because Slack is where the leakage is.
2. Mobile → Notion widget on your home screen pointed at Inbox. For the 9pm thought.
3. Desktop → global shortcut to a new Inbox row. Title only is fine — you triage at 15:30.

The rule that makes capture work: Inbox rows are allowed to be scruffy. The moment capture requires a category and an estimate, you will stop capturing and the system starts lying to you.

07 · Delivery pipeline

Eight stages, because every stage is a decision you have to service

Your proposed pipeline has ten stages. I would run eight — and the two I have removed were removed for a specific reason: "Blocked" and "Waiting for Client" are not states of the work, they are states of the queue. Mixing those into the status field fragments the board and hides where things actually are. They become a checkbox and a reason instead.

01

Inbox

Raw, uncategorised, ten seconds to create. Exists so that capture never competes with focus. Emptied daily.

02

Triaged

Category, priority, store scope and requester assigned. Exists so that "what is this and does it matter" is answered once, in a batch, not eleven separate times a day.

03

Ready

WIP 5

Passes the Definition of Ready: metric, baseline, estimate, assets, dependencies. Exists so that starting work never means discovering you cannot. The most commonly skipped stage and the most expensive one to skip.

04

In Progress

WIP 2

Actively being built. Exists to be small. Two is the limit — one deep item and one that can be picked up in a short gap.

05

In Review

WIP 3

QA passed on staging, plus any design or stakeholder sign-off. Merges your QA and Waiting-for-Client stages, because for a solo operator they are the same waiting room with different doors.

06

Staged for Release

Done, approved, sitting in a release. Exists because you deploy across five stores in batches — this is the stage your current board has no representation for, which is why "finished" and "live" blur.

07

Live

Deployed. Records which stores. Exists so that partial rollouts are visible rather than assumed complete.

08

Monitoring

Seven days of metric watch. Exists because on a five-store estate live is not done — regressions surface as mystery bugs three weeks later if nobody looks.

09

Closed

Result recorded, learning logged, release linked. Exists because a card closed without a result is a card you will re-litigate.

OFF-PIPELINE

Blocked ☑ · Parked

Blocked is a checkbox plus a reason on top of the real status — so you can still see that a thing is in review and blocked on design. Parked is a deliberate, reasoned deferral with a revisit quarter.

Why the Monitoring stage earns its place. Look at your release log: the DOM bloat fix improved LCP on some stores and coincided with a regression on others; the gift note was removed eleven days after release because orders were not printing. Both were discovered by accident. A seven-day monitoring window with the success metric already written on the card turns those from incidents into observations — and it is the only mechanism that makes the "Result" field get filled in, which is the whole basis of proving your value.

Who moves a card, and when

Only you. This is not a team pipeline and pretending otherwise adds ceremony with no benefit. What matters instead is when: Inbox→Triaged at 15:30 daily; Triaged→Ready during sprint planning; In Review→Staged when the release is cut; Live→Monitoring automatically; Monitoring→Closed at the Friday close-out. Four fixed moments, not a continuous obligation.

The rule that keeps it honest

A card cannot move to Ready without a success metric and a baseline, and cannot move to Closed without a result. If those two gates hold, everything else in this system can slip a little and the board will still be telling the truth. If they do not hold, none of the rest matters.

The six rules that make the week hold

1 · Only a P0 breaks a deep work block

Write this down and tell the client, so it is a policy rather than you being unresponsive. Everything else waits for 15:30 — which, from London's point of view, is still before lunch on the same day. You lose almost nothing in responsiveness and gain the entire morning.

2 · The 15:30 handover, four lines, every day

Shipped · Doing tomorrow · Blocked on · Need a decision by. Posted to Slack before London opens. This is what substitutes for being present, and it is why nobody needs to chase you. Ten minutes a day buys back the interruptions.

3 · Tuesday is a no-meeting day

One day a fortnight has the fortnightly meeting. The rest of Tuesday, and all of Tuesday on off-weeks, holds nothing scheduled. Protect it the way you would protect a launch date.

4 · Research is one timeboxed hour

Friday 15:00–16:00. Emerging search trends, Shopify releases, competitor moves, the agentic commerce question. Output is one row in Learnings. Timeboxed because open-ended research is either zero hours or five, never one.

5 · The reactive lane is a place, not an interruption

90 minutes a day, after the deep block. Bugs and small requests queue into it. If the lane overflows three days running, that is not a bad week — that is a signal, and it goes in the sprint retro and then to Guy with numbers.

6 · Never plan into the buffer

Friday afternoon and the last 2h of the sprint are structurally empty. If the week went well they become debt, research or getting ahead. If it went badly they absorb the damage. The moment you plan work into them, you no longer have a plan — you have a hope.

09 · Capacity planning

Where forty hours actually go — in four different kinds of week

A single capacity split is wrong for eleven months of the year, because an August week and a November week are not the same job. Four modes, each summing to 40 hours. Switch between them to see what has to give.

The reasoning behind the numbers

Why reactive gets 20%, not 10%

You described days where urgent development, bug fixes or general site requests consume the whole eight hours. If that happens even once a fortnight, the average is already 8h a week — and it is almost certainly higher than that, because small interruptions do not register as "a day lost" but cost the same. Budgeting 8h is not pessimism; it is the only way the other 32 hours become believable. Track it in the Sprint database and correct the number after six sprints with real data.

Why planned build is only 16h

40 minus 8 reactive, 5 release and QA across five stores, 4 trading operations, 5 steering and 2 buffer. This is the number that surprises people, and it is the most important line in the document: the organisation believes it is buying 40 hours of roadmap delivery a week and it is buying 16. Everything else is real work that has to happen and currently has no name.

Why 5h for release and QA

Your release log shows two to four deploys per store per month, with the same change replicated across five storefronts on different days. Each cycle is a pre-flight check, a deploy, a post-deploy verification and a metric read. Once the shared theme lands this falls to about 2h — which is 3 hours a week returned to build, or roughly 150 hours a year. That is the business case for the theme in one sentence.

Why commit to 80%, not 89%

The current H2 plan runs at 89% utilisation. Above roughly 80%, waiting time grows faster than the work does — one disrupted week does not cost one week, it costs three, because everything behind it queues. Commit 26h of the 32h available in a two-week sprint. You will finish more work committing to less, which is counter-intuitive and consistently true.

The escalation trigger — set it now

If reactive work exceeds 8h/week for three consecutive weeks, that goes to Guy as a number, not a feeling. "Reactive work has run at 13h a week for three weeks; that is 5h a week out of roadmap delivery, which is why the multibuy date has moved." Pre-agreeing the trigger means raising it is a process step, not a complaint — and the Sprint database produces the evidence automatically.

The one place this model breaks

The campaign week has only 1 hour of buffer and 4 hours of planned build. That is a real risk and it is deliberate — October cannot be made bigger. The mitigation is not to squeeze it further but to finish the AOV mechanics before October, which is exactly why multibuy sits in Sprint 3 and the gift builder in Sprint 5 rather than later.

10 · Sprint planning

Two weeks, four ceremonies, and one process change per sprint

Solo sprint mechanics fail for the opposite reason team sprints do. A team over-ceremonies; a solo operator under-ceremonies until the sprint is just a fortnight with a name. The fix is to make the ceremonies short, written and non-optional — and to make each retro produce exactly one change, which is the mechanism by which the system improves rather than decays.

Cadence

Sprint lengthTwo weeks. One week spends 20% of itself on ceremony for one person. Four weeks is too long to correct course with a freeze date approaching.
PlanningMonday week 1, 90 min, solo but written. Output posted to Slack: what is committed, what is not, and the one thing that will slip first if the week goes wrong.
Mid-sprint checkThursday week 1, 15 min. One question: am I going to make this? If not, cut now. Cutting on day 4 is planning; cutting on day 10 is failing.
ReviewFriday week 2, 45 min. What shipped, what moved, what the metric did. Feeds straight into the client report — the same 45 minutes serves both.
RetroSame day, 30 min, three questions (right). Ends with one process change written into the Sprint row. Not three. One that actually happens.
QuarterlyHalf a day. Roadmap review, programme roll-up, aging backlog purge, and the client-facing roadmap report.

The three retro questions

1. What ate time that was not on the board? This is how hidden work becomes visible. After three sprints the pattern is unmistakable.

2. What did I context-switch on most? Context switching is the largest invisible tax on a solo operator, and it is nearly always caused by one specific recurring thing.

3. What should have been a template? Anything done twice by hand. This question alone will build your template library over a quarter.

Answers go in the Sprint row. Reading six of them back-to-back in December is the most useful hour of your year.

Definition of Ready

A ticket cannot enter a sprint without all seven. This is the gate that prevents the most common solo failure: starting something and discovering on day two that you cannot finish it.

1 · Problem in one sentence — what is wrong or missing, for whom. If it takes a paragraph, it is two tickets.
2 · Success metric and current baseline — the number this is supposed to move, and what it is today. No baseline, no start.
3 · Store scope decided — which of the five, and whether it goes through the shared theme.
4 · Effort estimate in hours — yours, not anyone else's. Over 16h means break it up.
5 · Design assets attached, or explicitly not needed — the design team is your one real lever, and it only works with lead time.
6 · Dependencies named — apps, approvals, data, third parties. Named, and confirmed available.
7 · Rollback approach — mandatory for anything touching cart, checkout, pricing or navigation. One line is enough; nothing is not.

Definition of Done

Eight conditions. Note that four of them happen after the code ships — which is the part most solo operators skip and the part that makes the difference between shipping and improving.

1 · Shipped to every in-scope store — partial rollout is not done, it is drift.
2 · QA checklist passed — mobile and desktop, real device for anything on the purchase path.
3 · Tracking verified firing — if it is not measured it did not happen, and a broken event is worse than no event.
4 · Release logged — Releases database, with the LCP and INP read after deploy.
5 · Seven days monitored — no regression in the success metric or Core Web Vitals.
6 · Result recorded on the card — the actual number, against the baseline. Even when it is disappointing. Especially then.
7 · Learning logged — one row, one paragraph, if anything was discovered.
8 · Decision written into the card — not left in a comment thread.

13 · Risks and bottlenecks

Twelve ways this goes wrong, and what to do about each

Ordered by expected cost. Every one has a mitigation with a date, because a risk register without owners and dates is a list of worries.

Critical

1 · You are the single point of failure

Every credential, every deploy process, every undocumented decision and every Claude workflow sits with one person. There is no second Shopify admin who could ship a hotfix, and no written path for anyone to try. On 27 November this stops being an abstraction.

Mitigate Write a bus-factor pack by end of Sprint 2: access and credential list, deploy runbook, rollback steps per store, the five things most likely to break and how to fix them, and the AI workflows with how to run them. Two pages. Then identify one external Shopify developer, contract terms agreed, before 1 November — even if you never call them. The cost of having a name and a rate on file is an hour; the cost of finding one on Black Friday is the day.
Critical

GSC reporting is broken after the AI optimisation deployment. There are no signed-off CVR, AOV or RPV baselines per store. Every success metric in the job spec is a number, and right now none of them has a credible "before". This is both a commercial risk and a personal one.
